# Risks and claims to avoid: marketing online consultations on NextSlot (South Africa)

**Status: research notes, not legal advice.** South African law here is uncertain in places, and several sources below are law-firm or vendor blogs rather than primary texts. Verify against the Act, the Regulations, the regulator's own publications and the current codes before relying on any wording.

**Access limits in this session:** the primary sites (acts.co.za, popia.co.za, whatsapp.com, arb.org.za) failed DNS resolution from the research environment, so no primary text was read. Quotations below come from search-result summaries and are marked as such. Allowlist these hosts and re-run the primary checks before publication.

**Scenario assumed:** independent providers offer online consultations. NextSlot takes bookings and stores client phone numbers. The tenant (provider) contacts the client on WhatsApp. NextSlot does not host the call.

## Key question 1: POPIA (storing and sharing client phone numbers, WhatsApp contact)

### Takeaway
Any WhatsApp message that markets a provider to a client needs prior consent, or the existing-customer exception, under POPIA s 69. Opting out after the fact does not cure a missing consent. Sharing phone numbers with providers is a transfer to other responsible parties, and the platform's role in that transfer needs to be decided and disclosed.

### Cited Findings
- POPIA s 69(1) generally bars unsolicited electronic direct marketing unless the data subject has consented or is an existing customer under s 69(3). WhatsApp is treated as electronic communication alongside SMS and email — [MJK Inc, "Electronic marketing (section 69)"](https://mjkinc.co.za/popia/electronic-marketing-section-69) (secondary, search summary).
- Section 69(4) requires marketing messages to identify the sender and give an address or contact route for opt-out requests — [MJK Inc](https://mjkinc.co.za/popia/electronic-marketing-section-69) (secondary, search summary).
- Opt-out is reported not to count as consent for unsolicited electronic marketing, per a regulation quoted by one source — [MJK Inc, "Regulations 2025 amendments"](https://mjkinc.co.za/popia/regulations-2025-amendments) (secondary; not verified against the Gazette).
- The Information Regulator found that offering an opt-out does not remedy a failure to obtain prior consent under s 69(1)(a) — Information Regulator enforcement notice, FT Rams Consulting, [PDF](https://inforegulator.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/FT-RAMS-CONSULTING-ENFORCEMENT-NOTICE-210224-1.pdf) (cited from search result; contents not fetched in this session).
- Bowmans reports that the Regulator is finalising a guidance note on transborder information flows under s 72. No final guidance was found in the search — [Bowmans, "Draft POPIA regulations released for comment"](https://bowmanslaw.com/insights/draft-popia-regulations-released-for-comment/) (secondary). Michalsons notes that POPIA prevails over any conflicting guidance note — [Michalsons, guidance note on cross-border transfers](https://www.michalsons.com/blog/guidance-note-on-cross-border-transfers-to-from-south-africa/77246) (secondary).
- Section 72 gives several grounds for cross-border transfers (adequate law, binding corporate rules, binding agreement, consent). One firm states there is no adequacy list and no Regulator approval for ordinary transfers; prior authorisation under s 57(1)(d) applies to special personal information sent to countries without adequate protection — [MJK Inc, cross-border transfers](https://mjkinc.co.za/popia/cross-border-transfers) (secondary).
- A Wits paper argues s 69 does not extend to telemarketing by voice call. This does not affect WhatsApp messages, which are clearly electronic — search result from [Wits WiredSpace](https://wiredspace.wits.ac.za/bitstreams/839320fa-e203-4b85-bb4a-d84041dedd93/download) (contested view; not verified).

### Inferences
- Consent for WhatsApp marketing should be a separate, unticked opt-in, naming the provider. Pre-ticked boxes or terms-of-service acceptance are unlikely to satisfy s 69 (inference from s 69 and the opt-out finding above; no regulator text read in full).
- Telling a client "we'll WhatsApp you" on booking is a transactional or service message. Marketing content (offers, promotions, re-engagement) needs its own consent. Keep these separate in the flow and the copy (inference).
- The platform is likely a responsible party for the phone-number collection and a source of the transfer to providers. Its disclosure should say which party is responsible for which processing (inference; the s 1 role definitions were not read).
- Health data is likely special personal information under POPIA s 26, which tightens processing conditions for consultation-related data. This is from background knowledge; it was not verified in this session, and it should be checked against the Act.

### Gaps
- The text of s 69, s 72 and the 2025 Regulations was not read from a primary source (acts.co.za and popia.co.za did not resolve).
- The Information Regulator's own guidance on direct marketing and on s 72 was not reached (the regulator's site was not fetched).
- No source checked whether a consent form prescribed by the Regulations must be used verbatim.

## Key question 2: Advertising standards (ARB, formerly ASA)

### Takeaway
The Advertising Standards Authority no longer exists under that name (liquidated 2018). Its successor is the Advertising Regulatory Board (ARB). The core rules are that claims must be substantiated and not misleading, and objective claims need substantiation or a disclaimer. Specific clause numbers were not verified against the current code.

### Cited Findings
- The ASA was placed in liquidation in September 2018, and the ARB is its successor; current health claim rules sit in the ARB Code of Advertising Practice — [Bizcommunity/Daily Maverick search results](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2018-04-11-groundup-herbex-made-unproven-claims-about-weight-loss-products/) (secondary, search summary; ARB site not reachable).
- The code requires claims to be substantiated and not misleading. Clause 4.2.1 (misleading claims) is the provision most often applied, per a search summary — ARB Code, [arb.org.za](https://www.arb.org.za/) (not fetched; clause number unverified).
- Subjective puffery is generally permitted; objective claims need substantiation or an appropriate disclaimer. Comparative claims fall under clause 7 and must be substantiated and objectively verifiable — same secondary summary (unverified).
- Enforcement example: the ARB/ASA ruling against Herbex over unproven weight-loss claims — [Daily Maverick / GroundUp, 2018](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2018-04-11-groundup-herbex-made-unproven-claims-about-weight-loss-products/) and [GroundUp, "Herbex makes misleading statements after legal hammering"](https://groundup.news/article/herbex-makes-misleading-statements-after-legal-hammering/).
- A separate Cosmetic Advertising Code sits within the main code. It covers safety and efficacy of cosmetics — [ARB, Legacy Cosmetic Advertising Code (2020)](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/legacy-cosmetic-advertising-code-of-practice-(2020).pdf) (listed in search results; not fetched). Not directly relevant unless providers sell cosmetics.

### Inferences
- Testimonials, "results" claims and "verified" badges are the most likely ARB-sensitive items. Each needs substantiation on file (inference from the substantiation rule; the code's testimonial clause was not read).
- Medicine-related claims sit in a separate regime (see Key question 3). ARB jurisdiction over medicines advertising is contested in the sources found.

### Gaps
- Current clause text and any testimonial or endorsement clause were not retrieved.
- Whether the ARB reviews platform marketing (rather than the advertiser's own copy) was not established.

## Key question 3: Health and professional claims (HPCSA, SAHPRA)

### Takeaway
Healthcare providers' advertising is restricted by the HPCSA's ethical rules, including the ban on canvassing and touting under Rule 3(2). The HPCSA's telehealth guidance (Booklet 10, 2021 revision) applies to online consultations. Medicine advertising is governed by SAHPRA and Medicines Act regulations. Caution is needed for any consultation that could be read as diagnosis, treatment or prescribing.

### Cited Findings
- Rule 3(2) restricts canvassing, meaning drawing attention to one's services through verbal, printed or electronic media. It is listed in Schedule 1 of the Competition Act as a rule the HPCSA sought an exemption from — [Competition Commission notice, via search result](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/202503/52199bn742.pdf) (not fetched; contents unverified). A 2024 academic article argues the HPCSA should give clearer guidance on social media marketing — [WiredSpace, Wits](https://wiredspace.wits.ac.za/items/c3010a81-644c-4be7-87aa-097d7501cc4b).
- The HPCSA's Business Practices Policy (March 2024) is the document to check for advertising and practice-promotion rules — [HPCSA Business Practices Policy, March 2024](https://www.hpcsa.co.za/Content/upload/professional_practice/business_practice/Business_Practices_Policy_March_2024.pdf) (listed in search results; not fetched).
- Telehealth (Booklet 10, Dec 2021 revision): a relationship is not required before a telehealth consultation; practitioners are held to the same standard as in-person care; treatment or prescriptions based solely on an online questionnaire do not meet the standard of care; over-servicing and perverse incentives are cautioned against — [HPCSA telehealth summary, Mediclinic](https://www.mediclinic.co.za/content/dam/mc-sa-corporate/telehealth-pages/HPCSA%20SUMMARY%20TELEHEALTH.pdf) and [HPCSA blogs, telehealth guidelines](https://www.hpcsa-blogs.co.za/hpcsa-telehealth-guidelines/) (both secondary).
- One secondary source reports that the HPCSA advises against using social media platforms such as WhatsApp for telehealth consultations. This is a single, unverified report; it is directly relevant to the WhatsApp flow — same secondary summaries (unverified against Booklet 10).
- Medicines: SAHPRA's "Guidelines for Advertisement of Medicines and Health Products" exist — [SAHPRA](https://sahpra.org.za/document/guidelines-for-advertisement-of-medicines-and-health-products). Regulation 42 of the General Regulations sets further advertising rules; one source says Schedule 2–6 medicines may only be advertised to health professionals — [CMS Expert Guide, South Africa](https://cms.law/en/int/expert-guides/cms-expert-guide-to-advertising-of-medicines-and-medical-devices/south-africa) (secondary).
- Section 29 of the Medicines Act makes it an offence to make a false or misleading statement about a medicine, medical device or IVD (quoted in a search summary; not verified). SAHPRA has said advertising of unregistered medicines is "taken very seriously" — [EWN, Nov 2025](https://ewn.co.za/2025/11/17/sahpra-warns-of-harsh-legal-repercussions-for-distributors-of-unauthorised-medicines) (secondary).

### Sectors needing caution (from the scenario, with reasons)

| Sector | Why it is sensitive | Source type |
|---|---|---|
| Medical doctors, nurses (telehealth, prescribing) | Booklet 10 standard of care; no prescribing from questionnaire alone | HPCSA summaries (secondary) |
| Psychologists, counsellors, therapists | Therapeutic claims, crisis situations, confidentiality. Specific HPCSA rules for psychology not verified | Gap |
| Pharmacists, pharmacy services, medicine sales | Medicines Act s 29 and Reg 42; SAHPRA guidelines | SAHPRA, CMS (secondary) |
| Supplements, complementary or alternative medicine, weight loss | Unproven claims; enforcement precedent (Herbex) | Daily Maverick / GroundUp (secondary) |
| Dietitians and nutrition | Health claims about diet; HPCSA rules not verified | Gap |
| Fertility, mental health, paediatric, emergency-adjacent services | Higher harm if misread; not researched in this session | Gap |
| Legal and financial advice | Not researched in this session. Check the relevant professional body and FSCA/LPC rules | Gap |

### Inferences
- Any copy that reads as diagnosis, treatment, prescribing, or "cure" puts the provider, and possibly NextSlot as the advertiser of the service, at risk under the HPCSA and SAHPRA rules. Treat these as off-limits in platform copy.
- A WhatsApp-first flow for clinical consultations is likely to draw HPCSA scrutiny. This is the highest-risk design point in the scenario, given the single report that the HPCSA advises against WhatsApp for telehealth.
- Platform promotion of "consult a doctor/therapist" with listing-style copy may be read as canvassing (Rule 3(2)) if NextSlot is treated as acting for the provider. This is a legal question, not settled here.

### Gaps
- Exact Rule 3 and Booklet 10 text on advertising and on messaging channels was not read.
- No source was found on HPCSA rules for psychology, dietetics or other allied professions.
- The question of whether a booking platform's marketing is the provider's advertising is not answered by any source found.

## Key question 4: Consumer Protection Act 68 of 2008

### Takeaway
Section 41 is the central prohibition on false, misleading or deceptive representations about material facts, and it carries a duty to correct consumer misapprehensions. Section 29 extends this to marketing likely to imply a false representation. The most likely exposure is copy that implies NextSlot performs services it does not perform (hosting, recording, verifying).

### Cited Findings
- s 41(1)(a): a supplier may not, by words or conduct, directly or indirectly convey a false, misleading or deceptive message about a material fact. Quoted in a 2024 National Consumer Tribunal case — [NCT, SAFLII 2024/44](https://www.saflii.org/za/cases/ZANCT/2024/44.html) (search result; not fetched). Statutory text: [acts.co.za, s 41](https://acts.co.za/consumer-protection/41_false_misleading_or_deceptive_representations) (search result; site not reachable).
- s 41(1)(c): a duty to correct a consumer's apparent misapprehension where it amounts to a misleading representation — same search summary.
- s 41(3): certain statements are deemed misleading without further proof, including falsely claiming or implying approval or registration — same search summary.
- Commentary says s 41 reaches exaggeration, ambiguity and non-disclosure where withholding a fact is deceptive — [Polity, 2025](https://polity.org.za/article/the-consumer-protection-act-and-its-impact-on-advertising-and-marketing-in-south-africa-2025-02-28) (secondary).
- s 29: marketing that is reasonably likely to imply a false or misleading representation under s 41 is prohibited — same secondary summary.

### Inferences
- Implying that NextSlot verifies providers, hosts calls or holds records would be a material fact about the service. Those claims are the main CPA exposure (inference).
- Describing a provider as "verified" or "qualified" when NextSlot has not checked credentials falls under s 41(3)'s approval/registration limb if it implies a registration check (inference).

### Gaps
- Primary statutory text was not read in full.
- No NCT decision directly on online consultation platforms was found.

## Key question 5: Claims that could mislead (NextSlot's role)

### Takeaway
The highest-risk claims are the ones that blur the line between NextSlot as a booking platform and the provider as the clinician. Each must state who hosts, records, verifies and is responsible. These points come from CPA s 41, the ARB substantiation rule and the HPCSA rules, applied to the scenario. No source addressed this exact situation.

### Cited Findings
- None of the sources found address platform liability for a third-party consultation. This is a gap, not a finding.

### Inferences
Risk table for copy (applies CPA s 41, ARB substantiation and HPCSA/SAHPRA rules to this scenario):

| Risk | Source basis | Avoid (example wording) | Safer wording (example) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Implying NextSlot hosts the call | CPA s 41(1)(a) | "NextSlot video consults", "Our secure video room" | "Book a consultation with [Provider]. The call is run by the provider on their own video link. NextSlot does not host or record calls." |
| Implying NextSlot records or holds clinical notes | CPA s 41(1)(a); POPIA s 26 (special personal info, background) | "Your consultation history is stored on NextSlot" | "NextSlot stores booking details only. Clinical notes, if any, are held by the provider under their own obligations." (confirm with the actual data flow) |
| Implying NextSlot verifies qualifications | CPA s 41(1)(a) and s 41(3) on approval/registration | "Verified doctors", "Vetted specialists", "Qualified professionals" | "Providers list their own registration details. NextSlot does not verify qualifications. Check registration with the relevant council, for example the HPCSA register." |
| Implying NextSlot is responsible for advice | CPA s 41; HPCSA standard of care | "Our doctors will fix your problem", "Get your prescription online" | "Discuss your concern with a provider. Advice is theirs alone. Not a substitute for emergency care." |
| Diagnosis or treatment claims | HPCSA Booklet 10; Medicines Act s 29 | "Diagnose and treat from home", "Cure anxiety in one session" | "General consultation. Not a diagnosis or treatment guarantee." |
| Unverifiable outcomes or testimonials | ARB substantiation rule | "Clients get results in 3 sessions", "Reviews from thousands of happy clients" with no basis | Only publish reviews that are collected and dated; label them as the client's own experience. Keep records. |
| Privacy or security guarantees | CPA s 41; POPIA security duty | "Completely private and secure WhatsApp consults" | "Your details are handled under our privacy policy. Read it before booking." Do not promise end-to-end or absolute security. |
| "Instant" or "24/7" availability | CPA s 41 | "Instant doctor 24/7" | Only where providers are actually available; show real availability. |
| WhatsApp contact framed as booking | Meta Business Messaging Policy; POPIA s 69 | "We'll WhatsApp you your consult link" with no disclosure | "[Provider name] will contact you on WhatsApp to confirm your appointment. Reply STOP to opt out of marketing messages." (Separate consent for marketing.) |
| Marketing messages without consent | POPIA s 69 | Pre-ticked box; "by booking you agree to offers" | Separate, unticked opt-in naming the provider; opt-out in every marketing message. |
| Emergency gap | HPCSA telehealth standard | Silence on emergencies | "Not for emergencies. In an emergency, call your local emergency number (in SA, 10177 for ambulance; background knowledge, verify) or go to the nearest emergency unit." |

## Key question 6: Meta / WhatsApp Business policy

### Takeaway
WhatsApp marketing requires opt-in before messaging. The opt-in must identify the business, and separate opt-ins per message category are recommended. Opt-outs must be honoured. Outside the 24-hour customer-service window, only approved templates may be sent. Whether NextSlot is a Business Solution Provider (and so bound by Meta's partner terms) is an open question for the platform, and the answer changes the analysis.

### Cited Findings
- Businesses must obtain opt-in before messaging people, and opt-in may be general rather than message-specific under the Business Messaging Policy (Nov 2024 update, per a summary) — [Meta, Get opt-in for WhatsApp](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/getting-opt-in) (search result; not fetched).
- The opt-in must identify the business by name and state that the person agrees to receive WhatsApp messages from it. The business is responsible for compliance with applicable law — same Meta page (search summary).
- Meta recommends separate opt-ins per category (marketing, transactional, one-time password) to reduce blocking — same Meta page (search summary).
- Outside the 24-hour customer-service window, only approved Message Templates may be sent — [Meta, Get opt-in / messaging docs](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/getting-opt-in) (search summary).
- WhatsApp Business Terms require businesses to honour and comply with requests to stop or opt out of messages — [WhatsApp Business Terms](https://www.whatsapp.com/legal/business-terms) (search result; not fetched).
- A third-party site's "Business Solutions Specific Terms" text could not be verified against Meta and is not relied on here — [SaleSmartly](https://www.salesmartly.com/en/blog/docs/whatsapp-prevent-account-blocked-operation-behavior-compliance).

### Inferences
- A tenant messaging a client on WhatsApp is a business-initiated message. It needs a valid opt-in, and marketing content needs a marketing opt-in, separate from the booking message.
- If the platform sends the WhatsApp messages through a Business Solution Provider, Meta's partner terms apply to the platform too. Check this before launch.

### Gaps
- Current Business Messaging Policy and partner terms were not read from Meta's pages (whatsapp.com and business pages did not resolve).
- Whether a platform-sent WhatsApp message to a client, on the provider's behalf, is permitted under the partner terms was not established.
